# Product Reasoning Log

This log captures how the product question changed during the study, including hypotheses I rejected and the reasoning behind the final concept.

## Starting observation

This study started with a longtime Xbox player explaining why he prefers Achievements to PlayStation Trophies.

His Gamerscore is roughly 90,000, accumulated across about 15 years. He described it as an easy representation of how much he has played over time and something he can compare with friends.

What interested me was that he isn't particularly completion-oriented. He sometimes pursues Achievements deliberately, but he will also abandon a game he isn't enjoying and move to something else.

That made me question whether Xbox and PlayStation were recognizing the same behavior but communicating it differently.

## Hypothesis 1: breadth vs. depth

My initial interpretation was:

> Xbox emphasizes breadth and accumulated gaming history, while PlayStation emphasizes depth and completion.

That was too simple.

Both platforms have individual accomplishments, weighted values, lifetime aggregation and social comparison. PlayStation also has Trophy Level, which I initially didn't know existed.

The underlying systems were more similar than I expected.

The more useful question became why their presentation could still produce different interpretations.

## 850/1000 vs. 42/50

I compared two hypothetical completion states:

- 850 / 1000 Gamerscore
- 42 / 50 Trophies

They represent almost the same percentage.

They didn't feel the same to me.

850/1000 felt like a score. I associated it with getting a grade and being comfortable with having done well without needing perfection.

42/50 made the eight missing accomplishments salient.

The difference became even stronger at 49/50. I would probably complete something annoying simply to eliminate the final missing trophy.

The Xbox player I spoke with said his decision wouldn't change. If the final Achievement required something he didn't value, he wouldn't do it.

### What this changed

The representation itself appeared capable of interacting with different player motivations.

That moved the study beyond comparing mechanics.

## Achievement quality matters

We also discussed which accomplishments actually felt meaningful.

The player I spoke with dislikes Achievements that feel disconnected from meaningful gameplay, particularly collectible requirements that exist primarily as a checklist.

His preferred accomplishment would be defeating the game's hardest optional boss.

Importantly, he already seeks those bosses out without an Achievement attached.

For him, beating the boss is part of what the game is about. The Achievement recognizes something he already values.

My own behavior would likely be different. I'm more motivated by exploration, collecting and completion than difficult combat.

### What this changed

An Achievement can recognize meaningful play, create a new goal, or simply incentivize additional behavior.

Those aren't necessarily equivalent product outcomes.

## Engagement does not automatically equal player value

This led to another question:

> If an Achievement causes someone to play for six additional hours, is that necessarily successful?

Initially, I wasn't sure.

The distinction became clearer when considering why those six hours happened.

A player might spend them mastering a difficult boss because the challenge itself is rewarding.

Another might spend them completing repetitive tasks they no longer enjoy because leaving the checklist unfinished bothers them.

Both produce additional engagement.

Only looking at engagement would flatten two very different experiences into the same outcome.

### Product implication

I didn't want the eventual solution to introduce another progression mechanic whose primary effect was getting players to chase a number.

## Completion vs. mastery

We created a thought experiment.

### Player A

- 80 hours played
- completed the story
- defeated the hardest optional bosses
- earned 70% of the trophies

### Player B

- played on an easier difficulty
- followed a completion guide
- collected every required item
- earned the Platinum

Without additional context, the trophy system makes Player B's completion immediately legible.

Knowing the underlying play history changes the interpretation.

I might consider Player A's accomplishments more representative of mastery even though Player B completed more of the trophy set.

### What this exposed

Completion and mastery overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A trophy system can accurately report completion without being a complete representation of accomplishment.

## Wrong turn: fixing Trophy Level

At this point I thought PlayStation's cumulative representation might be the problem.

I considered several explanations:

### Maybe Trophy Level needs better explanation

If players don't understand what the number represents, perhaps PlayStation should make its meaning more legible.

### Maybe the calculation should be simpler

A more understandable relationship between earned trophies and Trophy Level might make the aggregate more meaningful.

### Maybe "Level" is the problem

In games, level often communicates character strength or progression. I questioned whether applying that language to accumulated trophies caused players to infer something the metric couldn't support.

Research weakened each of these explanations.

PlayStation already provides a cumulative Trophy Level. Its calculation weights trophy grades, and Sony has redesigned its progression before. The word "Level" alone wasn't sufficient to explain the problem.

More importantly, improving comprehension still wouldn't make Trophy Level communicate mastery, difficulty, longevity or which accomplishments mattered to the player.

### Decision

Stop trying to redesign Trophy Level.

The problem I cared about wasn't actually Trophy Level.

## What does a lifetime number represent?

This produced a broader question.

A high Gamerscore can suggest extensive accumulated Achievement history.

A high Trophy Level can suggest extensive accumulated weighted trophy history.

A large Platinum count strongly communicates completion.

But none of those alone can faithfully answer:

- What kind of player is this?
- What are they particularly good at?
- What experiences mattered to them?
- Which accomplishments are they proud of?
- Did they pursue mastery, exploration, completion, breadth, or some combination?

That isn't necessarily a failure of the metrics.

Compression is their purpose.

The problem comes from expecting the compressed metric to communicate more than it actually measures.

## Reframing the problem

The study started with:

> Why does Gamerscore feel better to one Xbox player than PlayStation Trophies?

For a while, it became:

> How could PlayStation make Trophy Level more meaningful?

Neither was ultimately the product problem I wanted to solve.

The question became:

> **How might a gaming platform represent lifetime player accomplishment without reducing it to completion alone?**

## Solutions considered

### Better Trophy Level explanation

**Rejected as the primary solution.**

Improves comprehension but doesn't solve representation.

### Simplified Trophy Level

**Rejected.**

A more understandable aggregate is still an aggregate.

### Rename Trophy Level

**Rejected.**

The evidence wasn't strong enough that terminology was the underlying problem.

### New player identity or mastery score

**Rejected.**

This would create another reductive metric and require the platform to make increasingly subjective judgments about what constitutes mastery.

### Rich multidimensional player profile

**Not pursued.**

Potentially more representative, but too large and interpretive for the problem being solved.

### Player-curated verified accomplishments

**Pursued.**

Preserves existing systems while adding a small amount of player agency over representation.

## Final product decision

Keep the existing Trophy system.

Don't replace Trophy Level.

Don't introduce another universal score.

Instead, allow players to select a limited number of verified accomplishments from their PlayStation history to feature on their profile.

The distinction that ultimately mattered was:

> **The platform knows what happened.**

> **The player knows which of those things represents them.**

The platform remains responsible for ground truth.

The player gets control over emphasis.

## Remaining assumptions

The concept still needs player validation.

The biggest unanswered questions are:

1. Do players actually want to curate their accomplishments?
2. Do players with different motivations choose meaningfully different accomplishments?
3. Should the platform suggest accomplishments, or would that recreate the interpretation problem?
4. Do existing Trophy Level or Platinum-oriented players perceive a showcase as additive or unnecessary?
5. Is profile representation important enough to players to justify the additional interaction?

The concept is a hypothesis, not a validated solution.
